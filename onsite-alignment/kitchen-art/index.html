Art by room — Kitchen

Kitchen wall art: make the everyday vivid

The kitchen is the room you live in most and think about least. Which makes it the wall where art works hardest — seen at seven in the morning, at six in the evening, and every time someone opens the fridge.

Why here

The art you’ll look at most isn’t in your living room

Living-room art is looked at on purpose. Kitchen art is looked at by accident — while the kettle boils, while the pasta water comes up, while someone tells you about their day. Two thousand glances a year, give or take. That is the longest relationship you will have with any object in your home. It’s worth choosing something that holds up.

The practical part

Yes, art belongs in a kitchen

Acrylic-glass finishes wipe clean with a damp cloth — the print sits sealed behind the face. Keep any work at least 60 cm clear of a hob or steam source and treat it like a cabinet front.

LUMAS editions are printed for archival lightfastness. Avoid a wall in hard direct afternoon sun — the same rule that applies to any framed work, in any room.

Centre at eye level, around 145–150 cm from the floor. Above a counter, leave 25–30 cm clearance from the worktop. Over a dining table, 70–75 cm above the tabletop.

Lean it on a shelf, or use adhesive hanging strips for works under 60 cm. Nothing here needs a drill.

Artists

Three artists who suit this room

Isabelle Menin

Menin builds flowers that never sat in a vase. Layer over layer, until colour behaves like weather. In a kitchen that is mostly pale wood and off-white, one of these does the work of a window. It reads as pure colour from the doorway and dissolves into detail when you are standing at the counter with a knife, which is the only viewing distance that matters here. Nothing in it wears out. That is the whole test.

See all works by Isabelle Menin

Craig Alan

From across the room it is a shape. From two steps away it is hundreds of tiny figures on a beach, arranged into that shape. Craig Alan makes a work with two speeds, and a kitchen is the one room that gives you both: the glance from the door, and the long wait by the kettle. Guests find the second reading on their own and tell you about it, which is a low price for a conversation you did not have to start.

See all works by Craig Alan

Werner Pawlok

Pawlok photographs rooms that were left rather than staged: peeling ochre, a shutter half open, furniture nobody moved. The colours are already faded, so they sit under kitchen light without fighting it. Hang one where you eat rather than where you cook. It is a picture of another room, at eye level, across a table, and it holds a look for as long as you want to give it. Quiet, but not empty.
