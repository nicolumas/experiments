Jonathan Chritchley · Li River, Guilin, Study 2

A workspace is the only room you arrange around a state of mind

Every other room is organised around something the body does: sleeping, eating, receiving people. A workspace is organised around attention. The desk serves your hands, the chair serves your back, and the wall in front of you serves the part that is actually working.

Which means the wall is never neutral. Blank is also a decision. The only question is whether it was made deliberately.

The three walls

A desk decides what your eyes can reach

Sit down and there are only three walls that matter, and they are not doing the same job.

Plan view. The desk faces the looking wall; the camera on the monitor faces the seen wall behind you.

Choosing for repetition

You will see this wall two thousand hours a year

Most art is chosen the way it is encountered in a gallery, in a single, concentrated look. The wall opposite a desk is the opposite situation. You will glance at it thousands of times, mostly while thinking about something else.

Work that resolves immediately does not survive that. It becomes furniture by the second week. What survives is work with something still in reserve: a detail you hadn't noticed, a tonal shift that changes with the hour, a composition that reads differently when you are tired.

It is a different criterion from do I like it. It is: will I still be looking at it in a year?

Placement

Hung for someone sitting down

Height
The gallery convention centres a work at about 145 cm, standing eye level. At a desk your eye sits near 115 to 120 cm. If the piece is meant to be looked at while you work, centre it lower, around 125 to 135 cm. If it is meant to be seen behind you on a call, keep it high enough to clear your shoulders in frame.
Width above a desk
A work hung over a desk should read as belonging to it: roughly two-thirds of the desk's width, centred, with 25 to 30 cm of clear wall between desk surface and frame.
Distance
Small rooms need smaller work than the internet suggests. But one large piece beats three medium ones in almost every workspace. Fragmentation is what makes a small room feel smaller.

On camera

The wall everyone else sees

A generation of working life is now conducted in a rectangle roughly a metre wide, and most of what it contains is the wall behind you. It says something whether you chose it or not: bookcase, plant, blank plaster, all of it reads.

Two practical notes. A camera crops brutally: wide landscape works vanish at the edges, so a single vertical or square holds the frame better. And the piece needs to sit high enough to clear your shoulders, which usually means hanging it above the height you would choose for a wall you intend to look at.

Light

A workspace has one light source the room didn't plan for

Art in a workspace competes with a backlit screen for the same eyes, in the same hours. That has consequences for the surface as much as the image.

Matte museum papers hold their image under mixed daylight and screen light and never throw a reflection back: the safe choice opposite a window or beside a monitor. Acrylic glass gives depth and saturation, and belongs on a wall where no window or screen sits opposite it, usually the wall behind you. Framed under museum glass for work you will sit close to.

A last note on contrast: high-contrast work in the corner of your eye is legible as movement. Keep the quiet pieces near the screen and the loud one where you have to turn your head.

Artist spotlight

Hengki Koentjoro

Koentjoro photographs water, mist and forest in Indonesia at exposures long enough to remove every incident from them. What is left is tone: fifteen or twenty greys, arranged.

At close daily range this matters more than it sounds. Work with a strong subject asks you to look at the subject, and after three weeks you stop looking. Work made of tone asks nothing, and keeps changing with the light in the room. His editions are square and small, 60 or 70 centimetres, which is the format that hangs comfortably at a seated eye line and the one a camera does not crop.

More on workspace art

Questions worth answering before you hang anything

The gallery convention centres a work at about 145 cm, which is standing eye level. Seated at a desk your eye sits closer to 115 to 120 cm. If the piece is there to be looked at while you work, centre it lower, around 125 to 135 cm, and leave 25 to 30 cm of clear wall between the desk surface and the bottom of the frame. If it is there to be seen behind you on a call, hang it higher, so it clears your shoulders in frame.
Roughly two thirds of the desk's width, centred on the desk rather than on the wall. A 160 cm desk takes a work around 100 to 110 cm wide. One large piece beats three medium ones in almost every workspace: fragmentation is what makes a small room feel smaller.
A single strong vertical or square. Video framing crops brutally at the edges, so a wide landscape behind a seated person loses both ends and reads as a stripe. Hang it high enough to clear your shoulders, and prefer higher contrast, because compression flattens tonal work into grey.
Matte museum papers next to a window or a monitor. They hold their image under mixed daylight and screen light and never throw a reflection back. Acrylic glass gives depth and saturation and belongs where no window or screen sits opposite it, which in a workspace usually means the wall behind you.
One piece, genuinely small format, hung to mark the corner rather than to fill the wall. The job is a boundary: something that says the desk is deliberate and not overflow. Square formats around 40 to 60 cm do this without turning the corner into a second room.
Honestly: the evidence for specific productivity claims is thin, and we are not going to make one. What is well supported is that the visual environment affects mood and attention, and that long focus relieves close work. The defensible argument is simpler. You will spend more waking hours in front of this wall than in front of any other in your home. It is worth being deliberate about.
