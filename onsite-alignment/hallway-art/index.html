Hallway wall art: turn passing space into a gallery

The hallway is the only room you experience in motion: walking, arriving, passing through. That is exactly how a gallery is designed to be seen.

The argument

A hallway already has what a gallery needs

Walls without furniture

Nothing competes. In most homes the hallway holds the only wall not already occupied by a sofa, a bed or a table.

A fixed sightline

You approach a hallway work from a distance and along an axis. The view is composed before you get there.

Sequence, not a single view

Art in a corridor is read one work after another, at walking pace. Rhythm becomes part of the piece.

Five approaches

Five ways to hang a hallway

The single vertical statement

One tall work, centred, nothing else. The most forgiving choice for a narrow wall: it uses height, which hallways have, instead of width, which they don't.

The pair

Two works of the same size and frame, 5 to 8 cm apart, read as a single object. Useful when one work alone looks lost.

The run of three

Evenly spaced along the corridor. Repetition is what turns walking into viewing.

The salon wall

A dense group aligned on one axis. Best on the widest wall you have, usually the arrival wall, rarely the corridor itself.

The framed sightline

A work placed to be seen through a doorway from the next room. The door becomes a second frame, and the work belongs to both spaces.

Practical

The measurements, once

01

Hang the centre of the work at 145 cm from the floor. Eye level, not wall level.

02

In a hallway under 1.2 m wide, keep single works under 60 cm wide. You will never step back far enough.

03

Leave 5 to 8 cm between two frames. 8 to 12 cm for a run of three or more.

04

Above a console, the work should be 60 to 75% of the furniture width, with its bottom edge 15 to 25 cm above the surface.

05

On a salon wall, align everything on one horizontal axis and keep the outer perimeter straight. The gaps inside may vary.

06

On stairs, follow the handrail angle. Each work 145 cm above its own tread.

Conditions

Most hallways are dark. Plan for it.

Few hallways have a window. Matt and satin finishes hold detail in low light; high-gloss acrylic will mirror the ceiling lamp straight back at you.

Favour high-contrast and graphic work where daylight never reaches. Subtle tonal pieces need light you don't have here.

A picture light, or a spot directed at roughly 30 degrees, does more for a hallway than a brighter ceiling bulb ever will.
