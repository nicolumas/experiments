Open studio: a visit with Marianna Gefen

Marianna Gefen has been painting since she was five. Today, she teaches Fine Art herself. Her work appears in galleries around the world and in leading publications from The New York Times to Vogue. A conversation about her fear of the white page, the personality of old paper and why sometimes the best thing an artist can do is stop.

On Marianna Gefen's desk, everything has its place. In her work, chance does too.

Pencils, charcoal, and ink lie ready beside the same watercolour set she got on her fifth birthday. What you won't find is a blank page: Gefen prefers paper that already comes with a history. Every sheet she draws on has been somewhere first: a flea market in Spain, a stationery shop in the former East, a pad that belonged to a schoolchild fifty years ago.

We asked her to take us across the desk.

What do you work with?

Different pencils, charcoal, watercolours, ink, and the watercolour set I have been painting with since my fifth birthday. But the foundation is the paper. I am absolutely terrified of a blank sheet, and I love vintage paper, anything that already has a history.

Your subjects are almost always women.

I am a huge admirer of femininity. My dissertation explores feminism too, particularly the visual worlds of the 1960s, 1970s and 1980s. Those delicate lines combined with very strong colour, it simply works for me. I can draw men. Do I enjoy it? No.

Where do you find the paper?

I travel a lot. In Spain it was flea markets. In Berlin, old East German paper, school notebooks, pads. You are never starting from zero, there is already a personality in the sheet, a history. If someone posts that they have fifty pads, I buy them like a madwoman.

When is a work finished?

That is the most important point. I am always telling my students: do not kill the painting. It happens easily if you keep working on it for too long. What I have developed is a kind of instinct for the right moment to stop. And I stop very quickly.

How many works do you make at once?

Ten or twenty at a time. One day pencil, then the first layer of colour, another the next. Out of twenty I might like two. On a good day, perhaps as many as fifteen.

What do LUMAS collectors get with this edition?

A direct insight into how I work. We really chose my favourites, especially the ones where something unplanned happened: a hole, a mark. People think that mark was placed deliberately and ask what it means. But sometimes it is just a fingerprint. I find that incredibly amusing.

Your studio is strikingly tidy.

Yes, I need order around me. Chaos would distract me. My art is where I make room for what I cannot control.
