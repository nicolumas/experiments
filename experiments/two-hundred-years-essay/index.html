1826 → 2026

Some images outlive their time. We show them.

Around 1.8 trillion photographs are taken every day, and they disappear just as fast. The first photograph in history has been on view for two hundred years. Choosing the images that deserve that kind of life is what a gallery is for.

Meet the photographers

Two hundred years in eight dates.

1826 Saint-Loup-de-Varennes, France

View from the window at Le Gras

Nicéphore Niépce fixes the view from his workroom onto a pewter plate. The exposure takes days. The plate survives, and hangs today in the Harry Ransom Center in Austin, Texas.

1839 Paris, 19 August

The daguerreotype goes public

Louis Daguerre's process is announced before the French Academy of Sciences and given to the world. The date has been celebrated as World Photography Day ever since.

1888 Rochester, New York

Photography leaves the studio

The first Kodak ships loaded with film for a hundred pictures. You press the button, we do the rest: the camera moves into ordinary hands, and the snapshot is born.

1925 Wetzlar, Germany

The century learns to see

The Leica puts 35mm film in a coat pocket. Photojournalism is born, and Robert Lebeck would later carry a camera like this around the world.

Robert Lebeck, Moskau 1979 · from $599

1936 Kodachrome

The world in colour

Colour film arrives for everyone. Saturated, stable, unmistakable: the palette in which Slim Aarons photographed high society at the pool.

Slim Aarons, Lounging in Verbier · from $3,199

1948 Cambridge, Massachusetts

The instant image

Edwin Land's first Polaroid delivers a finished print in about a minute. It began with his daughter's question: why can't I see the picture now.

1975 Rochester, New York

The image becomes data

At Kodak, engineer Steven Sasson builds the first digital camera. It weighs four kilograms, records 0.01 megapixels to cassette tape, and changes everything that follows.

2026 The anniversary year

Photography turns two hundred

We mark the year with a curated anniversary collection and hangings in our galleries worldwide. The peak: World Photography Day, 19 August 2026.

For 200 years, we have been choosing which images stay.

LUMAS represents photographers who made the history we are celebrating. Three of them, in focus.

Slim Aarons

High society at the pool, photographed by the man who was invited.

Slim Aarons spent five decades among what he called attractive people doing attractive things in attractive places. The world he photographed is gone. The images stay.

Discover Slim Aarons

Bert Stern

In 1962, Vogue sent Bert Stern into a hotel room with Marilyn Monroe. The Last Sitting became one of the most famous portrait sessions ever made.

Stern photographed the icons of his century with the same directness, from Marilyn to Kate Moss. Both sittings hang in this collection.

Discover Bert Stern

Robert Lebeck

The twentieth century, photographed at close range.

Five decades of reportage for Germany's great magazines, made with the small fast cameras born in 1925. His Berlin and Moscow series show the stages of a divided century: theatres, embassies, monuments.
